1.3 Headline architecture decisions
1. One domain core, two shells. A modular monolith with the same business modules
runs on each branch node and in the cloud.
2. Command, event, projection. Every write is a command that emits events. Every
dashboard figure is a projection you can drill down to documents.
3. Branch hub. The local service pushes tickets, tasks and approvals to every screen over
the LAN in real time, and replicates to a standby node.
4. Outbound-only cloud link. The branch opens one secure connection to the cloud. No
inbound ports, no broker credentials on shop PCs.
5. Control plane versus data plane. Platform staff manage tenants, billing and releases in
a plane that cannot read sales, stock or patient data.
6. Approvals as a domain. Requests, policies, limits and delegations are tables, not
scattered if-statements. Remote approval works offline through a challenge and
response code.
7. Rules before models. Alerts and reorder logic are deterministic and show their
working.
8. The database is the last guard. Sales of expired, blocked or over-MRP stock fail inside
SQLite even if application code has a bug.
Labels. Proposed default marks a number I chose to start from and that Phase 0 must
calibrate. Assumption marks a belief about local shop practice that needs confirmation
with real shopkeepers. Everything else is design.

resistance, so licence and cover alerts go to the Owner only, and inspector mode is
something the Owner switches on. Second, vendor and review-site claims in the table are

any comparison.
Where we deliberately do not compete (unchanged from v2.0): insurance claims, e-

3. Role model, permissions and approvals
Five personas get five workspaces, but the law needs one more idea: a licensed
pharmacist is a credential a person holds, not a job. v3.0 therefore separates the job role
(what you do all day) from the credential (what the licence lets you approve), and moves
every approval into one policy-driven system.

3. The person who counts stock cannot approve that count.
4. The person who creates a purchase order cannot post the goods receipt against it
unless the shop has one staff member, and then the Owner is notified.
5. A cashier who requested a void cannot approve the refund.
6. Approving a limit-exceeding action leaves one audit record with both identities, old
and new values, and the channel used.
3.6 Approving when nobody is at the shop
Remote approval works in three channels, tried in order.
1. Local PIN. The approver stands at the terminal and types their own PIN.
2. Phone push. When the branch link is up, the request reaches the approver's phone
through the cloud channel and is answered with biometric or PIN.
3. Offline code (challenge and response). With no internet at the shop, the approver
opens the phone app, which works offline, and types the action type, the amount they
intend to approve and the 6-digit challenge the terminal shows. The app returns a 6-
digit response computed with a secret shared only with that branch. The terminal
recomputes it. If the cashier relays a different amount, the code fails.
Offline codes expire after 5 minutes, work once, lock after three wrong tries, are capped
by a value limit the Owner sets, and are flagged for review when the branch next syncs.
Keys are derived per approver and per branch, so one stolen key does not unlock other
branches.
3.7 The licensed-pharmacist credential
The credential record holds the registration number, issuing body, valid-until date and
who verified it. Four gates use it:
1. Prescription and controlled lines cannot be finalised unless a user holding a valid
credential is clocked in as pharmacist on duty and has approved the line. The Drugs
and Cosmetics Act 2023 is summarised as requiring supervision by a qualified person
in the sale of drugs; the Owner confirms the exact rule for the shop's licence class.
2. Cold-chain and controlled batch releases need the holder's approval.
3. Recall decisions are recorded under the holder's identity.
4. A credential nearing expiry warns the Owner 60 days ahead and suspends the gates at
expiry.

4. Cashier experience
The Cashier succeeds when a typical 5 to 8 item invoice takes under 2 seconds from last
scan to printed receipt, no customer disputes the change, and the drawer balances at
shift end. Everything else on the terminal stays out of the way. The Cashier is also the role
most exposed to blame, so controls are built to protect honest staff as much as to catch
dishonest ones.
4.1 Jobs to be done
1. Ring up items fast with the right batch and price, without thinking about FEFO or MRP.
2. Take payment in any mix of cash, card, bKash, Nagad or credit, and give correct change.
3. Handle returns, holds and discount requests without leaving the counter.
4. Know their own shift is clean: float counted in, drops logged, blind count out.
4.2 A shift from start to finish
1. Sign in with PIN. The terminal shows who is clocked in as pharmacist on duty. Auto-
lock after two minutes idle.
2. Open the shift. Count the opening float by denomination. The count is saved against
the cashier's name and the counter.
3. Serve. Scan items or accept the ticket sent from the dispensing counter. The terminal
picks the earliest-expiry batch and shows the shelf code so an assistant can fetch it.
4. Hold and recall. Park a cart when a customer steps away. Held carts do not reserve
stock beyond the session.
5. Take payment. One screen, any split. Finalising writes the stock movements and the
invoice in one transaction.
6. Drop cash. When the drawer passes the Owner's threshold, the terminal asks for a
drop. The Branch Manager co-signs. Retail guidance commonly sets a drop threshold
and, in busy stores, drops every two hours (Xenia).
7. Close the shift blind. Count cash by denomination. Only after submitting does the
terminal show expected cash and the variance. This removes the "count to the
number" shortcut that retail guidance describes.
8. Hand over. The next cashier opens with the counted float; the shift diary note travels
with it.

5. Inventory Manager experience
The Inventory Manager needs to open the app and see, in order, what to do next and what
it is worth. Instead of reports to interpret, the home screen is a ranked work queue where
every task says why it exists, the taka at risk, and the one action that clears it. This role
merges the v2.0 Store Keeper and Purchase Officer, so its capabilities can be split again in
shops that have two people.
5.1 Jobs to be done
1. Receive goods correctly and shelve them the same day.
2. Keep front shelves full without overstocking.
3. Catch expiry early enough to sell, move or return the stock.
4. Keep counts honest without closing the shop.
5. Order the right quantity from the right supplier at the right effective cost.

5.6 Procurement that respects expiry and real cost
A reorder draft uses recent sales rate, supplier lead time and safety days, then caps the
quantity so it can sell before it expires:
q = \max\bigl(0,\; r (L + P) + r\,s - h - o\bigr), \qquad q \le r\,(D - b) -
h - o
Here r is units sold per day, L lead time, P review period, s safety days (proposed default 3
for class A, 2 for B, 1 for C), h on hand, o on order, D shelf life remaining at expected
receipt, and b a buffer in days. The draft shows these inputs in plain words, so the
Inventory Manager can disagree.
Effective cost. Each supplier line can carry a trade discount and, where suppliers give
them, free-goods schemes. Effective cost per unit is price times (1 minus discount)
divided by (1 plus free quantity divided by bought quantity). Free-goods schemes are

11.4 Inter-branch transfer
1. The requesting Branch Manager raises a transfer request; if the sending branch has

approves.
2. The sender picks by FEFO, prints the note and dispatches. Stock moves to an in-transit

3. The receiver counts what arrived against the note. A short or damaged line opens a
discrepancy with photos; it is not silently adjusted.
4. transfer.received moves the stock into the receiver's RECEIVING location, where it
needs put-away like any other receipt.

Managers and the Owner. Because stock is never in two places at once, a transfer
cannot create phantom inventory.

14.3 UX standards that apply to every screen
1. One screen, one job. A screen answers a single question or completes a single task,
and the next action is the most visible thing on it.
2. Keyboard-first for the counter, touch-friendly for everything else. Every counter
action has a key; touch targets elsewhere are large enough for a busy hand.
3. Defaults do the work. The most common choice is preselected; the Cashier changes
things by exception.
4. Say what happened and what to do. Errors name the cause in plain words and offer
the next step ("This batch expired on 31 March. Choose another batch"), never a code
alone.
5. Undo before confirm. Reversible steps (hold a sale, remove a line) just happen. Only
irreversible ones ask for confirmation, and policy-gated ones route to approval.
6. Colour never carries meaning alone. Red, amber and green always come with an icon
and a word.
7. Calm density. Dense where speed matters (the counter, the work queue), roomy
where decisions matter (approvals, policy changes).
8. No dead ends offline. Any screen that needs the cloud says so, shows the last known
data with its age, and offers what can be done locally.
